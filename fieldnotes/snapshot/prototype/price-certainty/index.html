A steadier cost,ten years on.

In 2036 nobody reads a tariff: a household sets its comfort bounds once, an agent trades the home's flexibility all year, and a carrier turns the proceeds into one annual number.

A projection of 2036 with invented figures, not a forecast. Rosa, Marion and Yusuf are invented households, and no real utility, retailer, insurer or reinsurer shown here is a participant.

01 / The household we follow

Rosa can hand over a water heater.

She rents Flat 3B in a 1962 block, works nights at a care home and is paid fortnightly. The storage heaters are hard-wired and the landlord has twice said no to a heat pump, so an immersion tank and one socket are the whole list of things she is allowed to give.

      02 / Who is in the room

      Flexibility is the new capital.

      Everything a carrier can sell on a household's behalf depends on hardware that household can host, so the homes most exposed to a volatile market have the least to trade. Rosa carries this story; choose another and every screen after this recalculates.

      03 / The counterfactual

      The year, without any of this.

      Same home, same energy, three versions of 2036 — nothing hedged and nothing smoothed. The gap between them is the whole reason anybody would buy the product.

        06 / The thing it is not

        Budget billing is free, and it does not do this.

        Levelized billing averages last year's usage into a flat monthly payment and trues up at year end, passing every price rise through in full. Certainty changes the total; smoothing only changes when you pay it.

          08 / Where the swing lands

          Someone is short this.

          Push market prices up across the whole book and the shortfall climbs three layers in order: flexibility revenue, the captive's own capital, then the reinsurance tower. Past the top the warranty pays pro rata, which is the honest description of what a household holds.

          09 / The wrapper

          Two structures, two regulators.

          Settlement-only leaves the supplier untouched and, in the United States, looks very like an off-exchange swap sold to someone who cannot lawfully enter one. Supply-coupled gives that premise up: the carrier delivers the energy it dispatches.
